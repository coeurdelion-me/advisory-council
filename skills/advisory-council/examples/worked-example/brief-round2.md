# Brief: Larkspur Cycles bike-subscription proposal

*This is a synthetic example. Larkspur Cycles is invented and every figure below is made up. It
exists to show the shape of a brief, not to describe a real business.*

## Orientation

Larkspur Cycles is a two-shop bicycle retailer and repairer in a mid-sized town, owned by two
partners, with six staff. It sells and services commuter and family bikes. The partners are
considering a new offer and have written the proposal below. You are being asked to review it
before they commit money. You see only this document: no accounts, no customer data, no site
visit.

**Lexicon.** *Subscription*: a monthly fee for the use of a bike plus servicing, ending when the
customer cancels. *Fleet*: bikes the shop owns and rents out under subscriptions. *Utilisation*:
the share of fleet bikes out on subscription at any time.

## The charge

The partners ask one thing: **which of the three parts of this proposal is least worth its cost,
and what would make the whole proposal fail?** They have already decided they like it. A review
that agrees with them is of no use.

## The proposal

**Offer.** A monthly subscription of 39 pounds for a commuter bike, 59 pounds for a cargo or
family bike. It includes unlimited puncture repairs, a free service every four months, and
replacement within 48 hours if a bike is stolen and reported. Cancel at any time after the first
three months.

**Fleet and cost.** The shops would buy a starting fleet of 40 bikes at an average cost of 620
pounds each (24,800 pounds), financed from savings. Each bike is expected to last four years in
the fleet before sale as used stock at about 30 percent of cost. Servicing is done in the existing
workshops using existing staff time.

**Target.** 70 percent utilisation by the end of month 12 (28 bikes out), reached through three
channels: shop-window and in-store sales, a partnership with two local employers offering the
subscription as a staff benefit, and a social media campaign budgeted at 400 pounds a month.

**The three parts.**
1. The consumer subscription, as above.
2. The employer partnerships, which give each employer a 10 percent discount for 10 or more staff.
3. The theft-replacement guarantee, which the partners propose to self-insure rather than buy
   cover.

## Constraints the panel must design within

- The partners will commit at most 30,000 pounds in total and will not borrow.
- No new full-time hire. Workshop capacity today is about 6 hours a week of slack.
- The shops must keep selling and repairing as they do now; the subscription cannot cannibalise
  that revenue by more than a small fraction.

## What the partners want back

Name the part least worth its cost and say why. Name what would make the whole proposal fail and
the earliest sign you would watch for.

# Answers to the panel's round 1 questions

*Synthetic example. Larkspur Cycles and every figure here are invented. The answers are deliberately
a mix of measured, estimated and unknown, as real ones are.*

Answers are attributed by seat and question number. All four seats receive this whole document.

## Answered

**Workshop time per subscriber (Strategist 4, Operator 1, Devil's Advocate 4, The Client 2).**
Measured over the last three months of job tickets: a puncture takes 25 minutes of labour on
average and a four-monthly service 55 minutes. Assuming 2 punctures a year and 3 services a year
per bike, one subscriber costs about 3.5 hours a year, so 28 bikes cost about 98 hours a year, or
close to 2 hours a week at full utilisation. The 6 hours a week of slack was measured in a quiet
October to December; the summer repair rush has no measured slack, and that is a gap.

**Theft rate (Strategist 1, Operator 4, Devil's Advocate 5, The Client 3).** No local data. The
partners' own assumption is 6 percent of the fleet a year (about 2 to 3 bikes of 40), replacement
cost 620 pounds each, so about 1,500 pounds a year. They have not asked an insurer for a quote.
That is a gap.

**Cash after the fleet (Operator 3, Devil's Advocate 3).** 30,000 pounds minus the fleet at 24,800
leaves 5,200 pounds. Advertising at 400 pounds a month for 12 months is 4,800 pounds, so the
advertising alone uses almost all of it before any replacement, parts or the first cancellations.
No month-by-month cash forecast exists.

**Employers (Strategist 5, Operator 5, Devil's Advocate 1).** One employer (about 60 staff) has
said "we'd love to"; the other has not been approached beyond a conversation. Nothing is signed.
Realistically month-12 utilisation without them is whatever the shop-window and social channels
deliver, and there is no estimate for that.

**Cannibalisation (Strategist 8, Operator 2, The Client 5).** Baseline retail sales of commuter
bikes are about 55 a year, repair revenue about 70,000 pounds a year. No threshold for "small
fraction" has been set. The partners guess 5 percent of retail sales would convert to subscriptions.

## Deliberately not answered

- **Evidence for 70 percent utilisation by month 12 (Strategist 6, The Client 1, Devil's
  Advocate 1).** There is none. The number is a target, not a forecast. This is the most valuable
  unanswered question: the whole case rests on it, so weight your verdict accordingly.
- **Contribution per bike-month by channel (Devil's Advocate 2).** Not computed. We would be
  inventing it.
- **Breakeven utilisation (The Client 4).** Same reason.

## Our own failures while preparing this

The first draft of the proposal had the advertising budget as a monthly figure in one place and an
annual figure in another, and nobody noticed until preparing these answers. If the proposal's own
arithmetic slipped, treat its other numbers with matching care.

# Round 2: the verdict

You asked in round 1 what you needed to know. The answers are above. Now give your verdict.

Verification, one item at a time:
1. Is the theft-replacement guarantee affordable on the answers given? Rule on the self-insurance decision separately.
2. Does the 6 hours a week of workshop slack survive the summer?
3. Does the cash plan stay inside the 30,000 pound cap and the no-borrowing rule?

Then an open critique with no cap on the number of issues. End with the one part of the proposal
least worth its cost, what would make the whole proposal fail, and the earliest sign to watch for.
